01 A little intuition

What does a
90¢ prediction mean?

A binary contract pays $1 if its outcome happens, and $0 if it doesn’t. A price of 90¢ reads like a 90% chance. Over many comparable predictions, you would expect that outcome about nine times out of ten.

The interesting question lives in the gap between that expectation and what actually happened. Fees and execution complicate the step from probability to return.

THE BOTTLENECK

Waiting on one cursor.

Turning up the request rate wasn’t enough. Each cursor still had to wait for its previous page. Disjoint time-window shards let collection progress in parallel, while a shared rate limiter kept overall pacing in one place.

THE TRADEOFF

A very expensive index.

A primary-key index across hundreds of millions of trade IDs would exceed the memory budget. Vectorized batches made progress possible, with deduplication and a quality gate carrying the obligation to catch overlaps after a restart.

THE LESSON

Yes, the machine froze.

An uncapped analysis exhausted the box. The revised workflow bounds memory, contains heavy jobs in a kernel cgroup, and splits aggregation into time partitions with disk guards. The 12 GB DuckDB and 24 GB cgroup limits are configuration—not measured peak usage.

05 Notes in the margin

The caveats are
part of the result.

Some categories have a clearer signal than the pooled estimate. Fees matter. So do correlated outcomes, retrospective grouping, and the difference between a recorded fill and a trade you could actually make.

That’s what made this a worthwhile experiment.

A follow-up would investigate historical fee reconstruction, shifts in category composition, and the gap between observed fills and achievable execution. These are open research questions, not a promised roadmap.

What is the uncertainty showing?

Outcomes from the same event are correlated. The analysis resamples whole events, not individual contracts. Pooled curves use 400 bootstrap replicates; headline estimates use 1,000. These are 95% intervals. An interval crossing zero does not prove there is no effect; a positive interval from very few events deserves caution. Event counts cannot simply be added across groups. Category comparisons are exploratory; their intervals are not adjusted for testing multiple categories.

What does “maker” mean?

A maker posts an offer; a taker accepts one. The fee model uses series-specific schedules, multipliers, and rounding. Maker fees are zero for quadratic series and one quarter of the taker formula for series with maker fees. Unverified flat-fee series are excluded. The fee catalog is a snapshot, not a complete historical reconstruction. The assumed rounding-era boundary is approximate; the report includes a rounding sensitivity check. Its fee-drift exposure measure is 35.23% of panel contract volume on series with recorded fee changes. Role curves compare different observed samples, not a controlled change in fees.

What remains unresolved?

The before/after split is observational: publication cannot be isolated from changes in market mix or time. Final-volume liquidity groups are retrospective. Recorded trades do not establish that a new participant could obtain the same fills, avoid adverse selection, or achieve these returns. The exact headline band is [80¢, 97¢); role curves retain full 5¢ bins through 95–99¢.

Why did the original report say “GO”?

The original research rule required a qualifying result in a liquid category. That is a different criterion from requiring the pooled interval to exclude zero. The saved GO is a research decision under that rule, not a recommendation to trade. Read the unchanged original verdict.
